Uniform Inference on Treatment Group Means

Author

David Malison

Last modified

July 30, 2026

This note derives confidence intervals for linear combinations of treatment group means using data from a randomized experiment. We model the data using a fixed potential outcomes framework and show that the confidence intervals satisfy a uniform coverage property. We also illustrate the confidence intervals’ finite-sample behavior using a Monte Carlo simulation.

Model

We index experimental units by \(i = 1, 2, \ldots\). Each unit is assigned to either treatment or control. Let \(D_i\) denote an observed treatment indicator, with \(D_i = 1\) if unit \(i\) is assigned to treatment and \(D_i = 0\) otherwise. We make the following assumption about the treatment assignment mechanism:

Assumption 1. \(D_1, D_2, \ldots\) are independent Bernoulli random variables with common treatment probability \(p\), and there exists \(\underline p>0\) such that \(\underline p\leq p\leq1-\underline p\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta\).

Assumption 1 excludes stratified designs that exactly assign a predetermined fraction of units to each treatment group. These designs are less common in online experiments, where treatment assignments are typically made sequentially as units enter the experiment. Assumption 1 also requires the treatment assignment probability \(p\) to be bounded away from zero and one. In online experiments, randomization is typically performed by hashing unit identifiers into a finite number of buckets divided between treatment groups, so Assumption 1 holds with \(\underline p\) equal to the reciprocal of the total number of buckets.

In addition to \(D_i\), we observe an outcome of interest \(Y_i\) that we model using potential outcomes. We condition on the potential outcomes and define estimands for the units in the experiment, making no claims about generalizability to a broader population. Analysts must therefore assess whether a particular experiment’s results generalize to other populations or contexts.

Let \(y_{i,d}\) denote unit \(i\)’s potential outcome under treatment \(d \in \{0, 1\}\). We assume \(Y_i\) is generated according to the following rule:

Assumption 2. The observed outcome \(Y_i\) satisfies

\[Y_i = D_i y_{i, 1} + (1 - D_i) y_{i, 0}. \tag{1}\]

Assumption 2 requires unit \(i\)’s observed outcome to depend only on unit \(i\)’s treatment assignment and not on the treatment assignments of other units. This is a form of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) and can be restrictive in some settings. For example, if \(i\) indexes user sessions, then Assumption 2 rules out treatment in one session changing potential outcomes in subsequent sessions. Such spillovers can often be handled by randomizing at a coarser level, but doing so lowers the number of experimental units and therefore usually reduces the experiment’s statistical power.

For \(d\in\{0,1\}\), define the potential outcome mean and centered potential outcome by \[ \overline y_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_{i,d}, \qquad u_{i,d,n} = y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}. \]

Collect the means and centered potential outcomes into the vectors \[ \overline{\mathbf y}_n = \begin{pmatrix} \overline y_{0,n} \\ \overline y_{1,n} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf u_{i,n} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{i,0,n} \\ u_{i,1,n} \end{pmatrix}, \] and define the covariance matrix

\[ \mathbf{S}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{u}_{i,n} \mathbf{u}_{i,n}' = \begin{pmatrix} s_{00,n}^2 & s_{01,n} \\ s_{01,n} & s_{11,n}^2 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{2}\]

Also collect the model primitives into the parameter vector

\[ \theta = \left( p,\{y_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^\infty,\{y_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^\infty \right) \]

and let \(\Theta \subseteq (0, 1) \times \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) denote the set of parameters under consideration. We impose the following regularity condition on \(\Theta\):

Assumption 3. There exists an index \(N\) for which \(s_{dd,n}^2>0\) for all \(n\geq N\), \(d\in\{0,1\}\), and \(\theta\in\Theta\). In addition, for each \(d\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \frac{|y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}|}{\sqrt n\,s_{dd,n}} =0. \]

Assumption 3 rules out dominant units by requiring that, within each treatment arm, no single unit accounts for a non-negligible share of the total potential outcome variation as the sample grows. In the Appendix, we show this assumption holds with arbitrarily high probability when potential outcomes are generated by a stationary ergodic process with finite, positive variances.

For \(\theta\in\Theta\), let \(\mathbb{P}_\theta\) denote the probability measure governing the observed sample \(\{(D_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) under the random assignment mechanism in Assumption 1 and the observation equation in Assumption 2. We let \(\mathcal{P} = \{\mathbb{P}_\theta: \theta \in \Theta\}\) denote the set of possible probability measures under consideration. We use the notation and auxiliary results from the uniform asymptotics note.

Inference

Target

In this note, we are interested in performing inference on a linear combination of the potential outcome means. Define

\[\psi_n = \mathbf{a}_n' \overline{\mathbf{y}}_n\]

for a nonzero, nonrandom coefficient vector \(\mathbf{a}_n = (a_{0,n}, a_{1,n})'\). Setting \(\mathbf{a}_n = (1, 0)'\) or \(\mathbf{a}_n = (0, 1)'\) yields the control or treatment group mean, respectively, while setting \(\mathbf{a}_n = (-1, 1)'\) yields the average treatment effect (ATE). Although each coefficient vector is nonrandom, we allow the coefficients to vary with \(n\) to accommodate other applications (see the sign-specific contrasts in the relative treatment effects note for an example).

For \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), let

\[C_{1-\alpha,n} = C_{1-\alpha,n}(D_1, \ldots, D_n, Y_1, \ldots, Y_n) \tag{3}\]

denote a confidence set for \(\psi_n\) with nominal coverage level \(1-\alpha\). The sequence \(\{C_{1-\alpha,n}\}_{n \geq 1}\) is asymptotically valid for \(\{\psi_n\}_{n\geq 1}\) over \(\Theta\) if

\[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \inf_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left(\psi_n \in C_{1-\alpha,n}\right) \geq 1 - \alpha. \tag{4}\]

Asymptotic validity permits finite-sample undercoverage, but requires the worst-case coverage shortfall to converge to zero.

Normal approximation

We estimate \(\psi_n\) by replacing the potential outcome means with the control and treatment group sample means. Let \(D_{i,1}=D_i\), \(D_{i,0}=1-D_i\), \(p_1=p\), and \(p_0=1-p\). For \(d\in\{0,1\}\), define \[ \overline D_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}. \] We restrict the formulas below to samples in which both treatment groups are nonempty and define the resulting quantities arbitrarily otherwise. This convention is asymptotically inconsequential, since \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \overline D_{0,n}=0 \text{ or } \overline D_{1,n}=0 \right) \leq \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \left(p_1^n+p_0^n\right) \leq \lim_{n\to\infty}2(1-\underline p)^n =0. \]

Define the sample means \[ \widehat y_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n\overline D_{d,n}} \sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}Y_i, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf y}_n = \begin{pmatrix} \widehat y_{0,n} \\ \widehat y_{1,n} \end{pmatrix} \tag{5}\]

and set

\[ \widehat{\psi}_n = \mathbf{a}_n'\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_n. \]

To describe the sampling behavior of \(\widehat\psi_n\), we first define

\[ \mathbf{b}_n = \begin{pmatrix} -\dfrac{a_{0,n}}{p_0} \\[2ex] \dfrac{a_{1,n}}{p_1} \end{pmatrix} \]

and

\[ X_{i,n} = \frac{D_i - p_1}{\sqrt{n}} \mathbf{b}_n'\mathbf{u}_{i,n}. \]

Under Assumption 1, \(\{X_{i,n}\}\) is a triangular array of row-wise independent, mean-zero random variables. The variance of the row-wise sum is

\[ \sigma_n^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{V}(X_{i,n}) = p_0p_1\mathbf{b}_n'\mathbf{S}_n\mathbf{b}_n. \tag{6}\]

Because the covariance terms in \(\mathbf{S}_n\) are not identified, we cannot estimate \(\sigma_n^2\) directly. Instead, we define \[ \widetilde{\sigma}_n^2 = p_0p_1 \left( \frac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{p_0} + \frac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{p_1} \right)^2. \tag{7}\] By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_n^2 &= a_{0,n}^2\frac{p_1}{p_0}s_{00,n}^2 + a_{1,n}^2\frac{p_0}{p_1}s_{11,n}^2 - 2a_{0,n}a_{1,n}s_{01,n} \\ &\leq a_{0,n}^2\frac{p_1}{p_0}s_{00,n}^2 + a_{1,n}^2\frac{p_0}{p_1}s_{11,n}^2 + 2|a_{0,n}a_{1,n}|s_{00,n}s_{11,n} \\ &= \widetilde{\sigma}_n^2. \end{aligned} \tag{8}\]

Since \(\widetilde{\sigma}_n^2\) does not contain the unidentified covariance term, we can estimate it using the observed within-group variances. Define

\[ \widehat{s}_{dd,n}^2 = \frac{1}{n\overline D_{d,n}} \sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}\left(Y_i-\widehat y_{d,n}\right)^2, \qquad d\in\{0,1\}, \tag{9}\] and set

\[ \widehat{\sigma}_n^2 = \overline D_{1,n}\overline D_{0,n} \left( \frac{|a_{0,n}|\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}} + \frac{|a_{1,n}|\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}} \right)^2. \tag{10}\] Since \(\mathbf{a}_n\neq\mathbf{0}\) and \(p_0,p_1>0\), Assumption 3 implies that \(\widetilde{\sigma}_n^2>0\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta\) and all sufficiently large \(n\). Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(n\), \[ \left\{\widehat\sigma_n=0\right\} \subseteq \left\{ \left| \frac{\widehat\sigma_n^2}{\widetilde\sigma_n^2}-1 \right| \geq1 \right\} \tag{11}\] In the Appendix, we show \[ \frac{\widehat{\sigma}_n^2}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n^2} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to} 1. \tag{12}\] Together with (11), this implies \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta(\widehat\sigma_n=0) =0. \] We therefore define ratios with denominator \(\widehat\sigma_n\) arbitrarily when \(\widehat\sigma_n=0\). The Appendix establishes the asymptotic linearization

\[ \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n}{\widehat{\sigma}_n}\right) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_{i,n}}{\widehat{\sigma}_n} + o_{\mathcal{P}}(1). \tag{13}\]

For all sufficiently large \(n\), define \(\omega_n\) as \[ \omega_n^2 = \frac{\sigma_n^2}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n^2} \tag{14}\] and let \(Z_n\) have distribution \(N(0,\omega_n^2)\). In the Appendix, we use the Lyapunov central limit theorem and Proposition 3 to show \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_{i,n}}{\widehat\sigma_n} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\approx} Z_n. \tag{15}\] Proposition 4 therefore implies

\[ \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n}{\widehat{\sigma}_n}\right) \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\approx} Z_n. \tag{16}\]

Confidence interval

We now form the confidence interval

\[ C_{1-\alpha,n} = \left[ \widehat{\psi}_n - z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_n^2}{n}}, \widehat{\psi}_n + z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_n^2}{n}} \right] \tag{17}\]

where \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) is the \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of the standard normal distribution. On samples for which \(\widehat\sigma_n>0\), this interval covers \(\psi_n\) if and only if

\[ \sqrt{n}\left|\frac{\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n}{\widehat{\sigma}_n}\right|\leq z_{1-\alpha/2}. \]

Let \(\Phi\) denote the standard normal distribution function and consider arbitrary sequences \(n_k\to\infty\) and \(\theta_k\in\Theta\). Since \(\omega_{n_k}^2\in[0,1]\), there is a further subsequence \(n_{k_j}\) along which \(\omega_{n_{k_j}}^2\to\omega^2\) for some \(\omega^2\in[0,1]\). If \(\omega>0\), then \(\omega_{n_{k_j}}>0\) for all sufficiently large \(j\), and \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{j\to\infty} \left| \mathbb{P}_{\theta_{k_j}}(Z_{n_{k_j}}\leq t) -\Phi(t/\omega) \right| &= \lim_{j\to\infty} \left| \Phi(t/\omega_{n_{k_j}}) -\Phi(t/\omega) \right| \\ &=0 \end{aligned} \] for every \(t\in\mathbb R\). If \(\omega=0\), then \[ \lim_{j\to\infty} \left| \mathbb{P}_{\theta_{k_j}}(Z_{n_{k_j}}\leq t) -\mathbf 1\{t>0\} \right| =0 \] for every \(t\neq0\). Part (i) of Proposition 3 and (16) therefore imply \[ \lim_{j\to\infty} \mathbb P_{\theta_{k_j}}\left( \sqrt{n_{k_j}} \frac{\widehat\psi_{n_{k_j}}-\psi_{n_{k_j}}} {\widehat\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \leq t \right) = \begin{cases} \Phi(t/\omega), & \omega>0, \\[1ex] \mathbf 1\{t>0\}, & \omega=0, \end{cases} \tag{18}\] for every \(t\in\mathbb R\) when \(\omega>0\) and every \(t\neq0\) when \(\omega=0\). Evaluating (18) at \(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\) and \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) and writing \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{j\to\infty} \mathbb{P}_{\theta_{k_j}}\left( \sqrt{n_{k_j}} \left| \frac{\widehat\psi_{n_{k_j}}-\psi_{n_{k_j}}} {\widehat\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \right| \leq z_{1-\alpha/2} \right) \\[1ex] &\qquad= \mathbb{P}\left(|\omega Z|\leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) \geq \mathbb{P}\left(|Z|\leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) =1-\alpha. \end{aligned} \tag{19}\] Now if

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty} \inf_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left(\psi_n \in C_{1-\alpha,n}\right) < 1-\alpha, \]

there would be an \(\epsilon>0\) and sequences \(n_k\to\infty\) and \(\theta_k\in\Theta\) along which

\[ \limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_k}\left(\psi_{n_k} \in C_{1-\alpha,n_k}\right) < 1-\alpha-\epsilon. \] But then no further subsequence could satisfy (19). Hence

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty} \inf_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left(\psi_n \in C_{1-\alpha,n}\right) \geq 1-\alpha. \tag{20}\]

Illustration

The animations below illustrate the sampling behavior of the confidence interval in (17). The left panel in each animation displays a fixed set of potential outcomes for the first 20 observations of a sample of \(n = 1{,}000\) units. In each frame, a new treatment assignment is drawn and the corresponding observed outcomes are shown. The potential outcomes do not change; all variation in the confidence intervals comes from the randomized assignment draws. The top-right panel displays the resulting estimator for the average treatment effect \(\psi_n = \overline y_{1,n} - \overline y_{0,n}\) and its corresponding \(95\%\) confidence interval, colored green when the interval contains \(\psi_n\) and red otherwise. The bottom panel shows the sampling distribution of the studentized estimator \(\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n)/\widehat{\sigma}_n\) across 50,000 assignment draws, together with the standard normal density.

In the first animation, \(y_{i,1} = y_{i,0} + 1\) for every \(i\). In this case, the conservative bound in (8) coincides with the true variance. The studentized estimator closely tracks the standard normal and the empirical coverage is close to the nominal \(95\%\) level.

The second animation uses \(y_{i,1} = -y_{i,0} + 1\). Here the two potential outcome sequences are negatively correlated, making the conservative variance bound in (8) loose. The studentized estimator is therefore under-dispersed relative to the standard normal, and the interval covers \(\psi_n\) more often than the nominal level.

Appendix

A mixture-model motivation for Assumption 3

Let \(\mathcal Q\) be a collection of probability laws on the path space \(\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}\times\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}\). Under each \(Q\in\mathcal Q\), suppose the coordinate process \(\{\mathbf{Y}_i\}_{i\geq1}\) is stationary and ergodic, where \(\mathbf{Y}_i=(Y_{i,0},Y_{i,1})'\). Let \(\Pi\) be a probability distribution on \(\mathcal Q\). Generate a potential outcome path by first drawing \(Q\sim\Pi\) and then drawing \(\mathbf Y\sim Q\). For each \(d\in\{0,1\}\), define the conditional mean and variance

\[ \mu_d(Q) = \mathbb{E}_Q[Y_{1,d}], \qquad V_d(Q) = \mathbb{V}_Q(Y_{1,d}), \]

and assume

\[ \mathbb{E}_Q[Y_{1,d}^2]<\infty, \qquad V_d(Q)>0 \]

for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\). The marginal law of the potential outcome path is the mixture

\[ \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}(A) = \int_{\mathcal Q}Q(A)\,\Pi(dQ). \]

Define the empirical mean and variance

\[ \overline Y_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nY_{i,d}, \qquad S_{dd,n}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(Y_{i,d}-\overline Y_{d,n}\right)^2. \]

Applying the ergodic theorem under \(Q\) to \(Y_{i,d}\) and \(Y_{i,d}^2\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\to\infty}\overline Y_{d,n} &=\mu_d(Q), \\ \lim_{n\to\infty}S_{dd,n}^2 &=V_d(Q) \end{aligned} \]

\(Q\)-almost surely for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\) and \(d\in\{0,1\}\).

We now show that no observation dominates the sample. Fix \(\delta>0\). Under any \(Q\in\mathcal Q\), stationarity and the union bound give, for every \(m\in\mathbb N\),

\[ Q\left( \max_{1\leq i\leq2^m}|Y_{i,d}| >\delta 2^{m/2} \right) \leq 2^m Q\left( Y_{1,d}^2>\delta^2 2^m \right). \]

The tail-integral formula and the second-moment assumption give

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{m=1}^\infty 2^m Q\left( Y_{1,d}^2>\delta^2 2^m \right) &\leq \frac{2}{\delta^2} \int_0^\infty Q\left(Y_{1,d}^2>t\right)\,dt \\ &= \frac{2}{\delta^2} \mathbb E_Q\left[Y_{1,d}^2\right] <\infty. \end{aligned} \]

The Borel–Cantelli lemma therefore gives

\[ \limsup_{m\to\infty} \frac{1}{2^{m/2}} \max_{1\leq i\leq2^m}|Y_{i,d}| \leq\delta \]

\(Q\)-almost surely. Applying this result with \(\delta=1/r\) for every \(r\in \mathbb{N}\) and taking a countable intersection gives

\[ \lim_{m\to\infty} \frac{1}{2^{m/2}} \max_{1\leq i\leq2^m}|Y_{i,d}| =0 \]

\(Q\)-almost surely for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\). If \(2^{m-1}<n\leq2^m\), then \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt n} \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Y_{i,d}| \leq \sqrt2 \frac{1}{2^{m/2}} \max_{1\leq i\leq2^m}|Y_{i,d}|, \] so the same convergence holds along the full sequence \(n\to\infty\). Since \(\overline Y_{d,n}\to\mu_d(Q)\) \(Q\)-almost surely, \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt n} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \left|Y_{i,d}-\overline Y_{d,n}\right| =0 \tag{21}\] \(Q\)-almost surely for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\) and \(d\in\{0,1\}\). The convergence event in (21) is measurable and has \(Q\)-probability one for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\). It therefore has probability one under the mixture law \(\mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}\) for each \(d\in\{0,1\}\).

We next show that, under the mixture law, each empirical variance converges almost surely to a strictly positive, measurable function of the potential outcome path. Write a generic path as \[ \mathbf y = \left( \{y_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^\infty, \{y_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^\infty \right). \] For \(d\in\{0,1\}\) and every path \(\mathbf y\), define the extended-real function \[ v_d(\mathbf y) = \limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}\right)^2. \] Each finite-sample empirical variance is measurable, so \(v_d\) is measurable. For every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\), the ergodic theorem implies that the empirical variance converges \(Q\)-almost surely to \(V_d(Q)>0\). Whenever this convergence occurs, the limit equals the limsup \(v_d(\mathbf Y)\). Let \(A_d\) be the path event on which the empirical variance converges to \(v_d(\mathbf Y)>0\). This event is measurable and satisfies \(Q(A_d)=1\) for every \(Q\in\mathcal Q\). Therefore

\[ \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}(A_d) = \int_{\mathcal Q}Q(A_d)\,\Pi(dQ) =1, \]

which proves \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(Y_{i,d}-\overline Y_{d,n}\right)^2 =v_d(\mathbf Y)>0 \tag{22}\] \(\mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}\)-almost surely for each \(d\in\{0,1\}\).

Now fix \(\epsilon>0\) and let \(\underline v(\mathbf y)=\min\{v_0(\mathbf y),v_1(\mathbf y)\}\). Since \(\underline v(\mathbf{Y})>0\) \(\mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}\)-almost surely, continuity from below gives a constant \(c>0\) such that

\[ \mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left(\underline v(\mathbf{Y})\geq c\right) >1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \tag{23}\]

Applying Egorov’s theorem to the convergences in (21) and (22), simultaneously for \(d=0,1\), gives a measurable set \(E'\) with

\[ \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}(E')>1-\frac{\epsilon}{2} \]

on which the empirical-variance and centered-maximum convergences are uniform for both \(d=0,1\). Now define

\[ E = E'\cap \left\{\mathbf y:\underline v(\mathbf y)\geq c\right\}. \]

By (23) and the union bound,

\[ \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}(E) \geq \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}(E') + \mathbb P_{\mathbf Y}\left(\underline v(\mathbf Y)\geq c\right) -1 >1-\epsilon. \]

Since \(E\subseteq E'\), for each \(d\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\mathbf y\in E} \left| \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}\right)^2 -v_d(\mathbf y) \right| &=0, \\ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\mathbf y\in E} \frac{1}{\sqrt n} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \left|y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}\right| &=0. \end{aligned} \tag{24}\]

Let \(\Theta\) be the set of parameters whose treatment probability satisfies Assumption 1 and whose potential outcome path lies in \(E\). The first convergence in (24) implies that, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \inf_{\theta\in\Theta}s_{dd,n}^2 \geq \frac{c}{2}>0 \]

for each \(d\in\{0,1\}\). Moreover,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \frac{|y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}|} {\sqrt n\,s_{dd,n}} &\leq \left(\frac{2}{c}\right)^{1/2} \sup_{\mathbf y\in E} \frac{1}{\sqrt n} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \left|y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}\right|. \end{aligned} \]

The second convergence in (24) therefore gives

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \frac{|y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}|} {\sqrt n\,s_{dd,n}} =0. \]

Thus \(\Theta\) satisfies Assumption 3.

Estimator asymptotics

Notation and auxiliary lemma

Define

\[ \ell_{0,n}=\frac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{p_0}, \qquad \ell_{1,n}=\frac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{p_1}, \qquad L_n=\ell_{0,n}+\ell_{1,n}. \]

We will use the following lemma:

Lemma 1 For each \(d\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ \overline D_{d,n}\stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}p_d, \qquad \sqrt n(\overline D_{d,n}-p_d)=O_{\mathcal{P}}(1). \]

Proof. Under Assumption 1, \(D_{1,d},\ldots,D_{n,d}\) are independent Bernoulli random variables with probability \(p_d\). Hence

\[ \mathbb{E}_\theta(\overline D_{d,n})=p_d, \qquad \mathbb{V}_\theta(\overline D_{d,n})=\frac{p_dp_{1-d}}{n}. \]

For every \(\epsilon>0\), Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left|\overline D_{d,n}-p_d\right|>\epsilon \right) &\leq \frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \frac{p_dp_{1-d}}{n} \\ &\leq \frac{1}{4\epsilon^2 n} \\[2ex] &\to 0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(\overline D_{d,n}\stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}p_d\). Also, for every \(M>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left|\sqrt n(\overline D_{d,n}-p_d)\right|>M \right) &\leq \frac{1}{M^2} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}p_dp_{1-d} \\ &\leq \frac{1}{4M^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(\sqrt n(\overline D_{d,n}-p_d)=O_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\). \(\square\)

Derivation of (12)

We first show that, for \(d\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ \frac{\widehat{s}_{dd,n}^2}{s_{dd,n}^2} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}1. \tag{25}\] Observe that

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{s}_{dd,n}^2 &= \frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}^2}{\overline D_{d,n}} - \left(\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}}{\overline D_{d,n}}\right)^2 \\ &= \frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}^2 - p_d\,s_{dd,n}^2}{\overline D_{d,n}} + \frac{p_d\,s_{dd,n}^2}{\overline D_{d,n}} - \left(\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}}{\overline D_{d,n}}\right)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{26}\]

By Assumption 3, there exists an index \(N\) for which \(s_{dd,n}^2>0\) for all \(n\geq N\), \(d\in\{0,1\}\), and \(\theta\in\Theta\). Together with Lemma 1, Proposition 5, and Proposition 6, (25) will follow from (26) if we can show \[ \frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}^2 - p_d s_{dd,n}^2}{s_{dd,n}^2} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to} 0, \qquad \frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}}{s_{dd,n}} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to} 0. \tag{27}\]

The first term in (27) has mean zero and variance

\[ \frac{p_dp_{1-d}}{n^2 s_{dd,n}^4}\sum_{i=1}^n u_{i,d,n}^4 \leq \frac{p_dp_{1-d}}{n^2 s_{dd,n}^4} \max_{1 \leq i \leq n} u_{i,d,n}^2 \sum_{i=1}^n u_{i,d,n}^2. \]

Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n u_{i,d,n}^2=ns_{dd,n}^2\), we can write the right-hand side as

\[ \frac{p_dp_{1-d}}{n} \left(\frac{\max_{1 \leq i \leq n} u_{i,d,n}^2}{s_{dd,n}^2}\right) = p_dp_{1-d} \left(\frac{\max_{1 \leq i \leq n} |u_{i,d,n}|}{\sqrt{n} s_{dd,n}}\right)^2. \]

Since \(p_dp_{1-d}\leq 1/4\), for every \(\epsilon>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left| \frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nD_{i,d}u_{i,d,n}^2 - p_d s_{dd,n}^2}{s_{dd,n}^2} \right|> \epsilon \right) &\leq \frac{1}{4\epsilon^2} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \left(\frac{\max_{1 \leq i \leq n} |u_{i,d,n}|}{\sqrt{n} s_{dd,n}}\right)^2 \end{aligned} \] by Chebyshev’s inequality. Therefore the first term converges in \(\mathcal{P}\) to zero by Assumption 3. Since the second term in (27) has mean zero and variance \(p_dp_{1-d}/n\), it also converges in \(\mathcal{P}\) to zero by Chebyshev’s inequality. This proves (25).

Since \(\widehat{s}_{dd,n}\) and \(s_{dd,n}\) are nonnegative, Proposition 5 also gives

\[ \frac{\widehat{s}_{dd,n}}{s_{dd,n}} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}1. \tag{28}\]

Now by (7) and (10), we have \[ \frac{\widehat{\sigma}_n^2}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n^2} = \frac{\overline D_{1,n}\overline D_{0,n}}{p_1p_0} \left[ \frac{ \frac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}} + \frac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}} }{ \frac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{p_0} + \frac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{p_1} } \right]^2. \tag{29}\]

The unbracketed term converges to one in \(\mathcal{P}\) by Lemma 1 and Proposition 5. It therefore suffices to show that the bracketed ratio also converges to one. For all \(n \geq N\), \(L_n>0\) and the ratios \(\ell_{0,n}/L_n\) and \(\ell_{1,n}/L_n\) are nonnegative and sum to one. The difference between the bracketed ratio and one can therefore be bounded by \[ \begin{aligned} &\left| \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \left(\frac{p_0}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}}\right) + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \left(\frac{p_1}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}}\right) -1 \right| \\ &\leq \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \left| \frac{p_0}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}} -1 \right| + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \left| \frac{p_1}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}} -1 \right| \\ &\leq \left| \frac{p_0}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}} -1 \right| + \left| \frac{p_1}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}} -1 \right| \end{aligned} \] Thus, for every \(\epsilon>0\), \[ \begin{aligned} &\sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left| \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \left(\frac{p_0}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}}\right) + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \left(\frac{p_1}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}}\right) -1 \right|> \epsilon \right) \\ &\leq \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left| \frac{p_0}{\overline D_{0,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{00,n}}{s_{00,n}} -1 \right|> \epsilon/2 \right) + \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \left| \frac{p_1}{\overline D_{1,n}}\frac{\widehat{s}_{11,n}}{s_{11,n}} -1 \right|> \epsilon/2 \right) \end{aligned} \]

The two probabilities on the right-hand side tend to zero by (28), Lemma 1, Proposition 5, and Proposition 6. Therefore the bracketed ratio in (29) converges to one in \(\mathcal{P}\). Combining this with the convergence of the unbracketed term proves (12) by Proposition 5 and Proposition 6.

Derivation of (13)

Define \[ U_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i-p_1)(y_{i,d}-\overline y_{d,n}), \qquad d\in\{0,1\}. \]

Using \(\sum_{i=1}^n (y_{i,d} - \overline y_{d,n}) = 0\), we can write

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat y_{0,n} - \overline y_{0,n} &= -\frac{1}{n\overline D_{0,n}}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - p_1)(y_{i,0} - \overline y_{0,n})\\[2ex] &= -\dfrac{U_{0,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}}, \\[2ex] \widehat y_{1,n} - \overline y_{1,n} &= \frac{1}{n\overline D_{1,n}}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - p_1)(y_{i,1} - \overline y_{1,n}) \\[2ex] &= \dfrac{U_{1,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}} \end{aligned} \]

We therefore have

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n\right) &= \sqrt{n}\left(a_{0,n}\left(\widehat y_{0,n}-\overline y_{0,n}\right) + a_{1,n}\left(\widehat y_{1,n}-\overline y_{1,n}\right)\right) \\[2ex] &= \sqrt{n}\left(-\frac{a_{0,n} U_{0,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}} + \frac{a_{1,n} U_{1,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}}\right) \\[2ex] &= \sqrt{n}\left(-\frac{a_{0,n} U_{0,n}}{p_0} + \frac{a_{1,n} U_{1,n}}{p_1}\right) + \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{a_{0,n} U_{0,n}}{p_0} - \frac{a_{0,n} U_{0,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}} + \frac{a_{1,n} U_{1,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}} - \frac{a_{1,n} U_{1,n}}{p_1}\right) \\[2ex] &= \sum_{i=1}^n X_{i,n} + R_n, \end{aligned} \tag{30}\]

where

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i = 1}^n X_{i,n} &= \sqrt{n}\left(-\frac{a_{0,n} U_{0,n}}{p_0} + \frac{a_{1,n} U_{1,n}}{p_1}\right), \\[2ex] R_n &= -\sqrt{n}\left(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1\right) \left[ \frac{a_{0,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}p_0}U_{0,n} + \frac{a_{1,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}p_1}U_{1,n} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Dividing (30) by \(\widehat{\sigma}_n\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\widehat{\psi}_n-\psi_n}{\widehat{\sigma}_n}\right) &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_{i,n}}{\widehat{\sigma}_n} +\frac{R_n}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n} \frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_n}{\widehat{\sigma}_n}. \end{aligned} \tag{31}\]

Since (12) and Proposition 5 give \(\widetilde\sigma_n/\widehat\sigma_n\stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}1\), Proposition 1 shows that it suffices to prove \(R_n/\widetilde{\sigma}_n \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to} 0\).

Toward this end, observe that \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{|R_n|}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n} &= \frac{\left|\sqrt n(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1)\right|}{\sqrt{p_0p_1}L_n} \left| \frac{a_{0,n}}{\overline D_{0,n}p_0}U_{0,n} + \frac{a_{1,n}}{\overline D_{1,n}p_1}U_{1,n} \right| \\[2ex] &\leq \frac{\left|\sqrt n(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1)\right|}{\sqrt{p_0p_1}} \left[ \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \frac{1}{\overline D_{0,n}} \left|\frac{U_{0,n}}{s_{00,n}}\right| + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \frac{1}{\overline D_{1,n}} \left|\frac{U_{1,n}}{s_{11,n}}\right| \right]. \end{aligned} \]

On the event \(\left\{|\overline D_{1,n}-p_1|\leq\underline p/2\right\}\), \(\overline D_{0,n}\geq\underline p/2\) and \(\overline D_{1,n}\geq\underline p/2\), so \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{|R_n|}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n} &\leq C_{\underline p}\left|\sqrt n(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1)\right| \left[ \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \left|\frac{U_{0,n}}{s_{00,n}}\right| + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \left|\frac{U_{1,n}}{s_{11,n}}\right| \right], \end{aligned} \tag{32}\]

where

\[ C_{\underline p} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\underline p(1-\underline p)}\,\underline p}. \]

Therefore, for every \(\epsilon>0\), \[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \frac{|R_n|}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n} > \epsilon \right) &\leq \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}_\theta\left( C_{\underline p}\left|\sqrt n(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1)\right| \left[ \frac{\ell_{0,n}}{L_n} \left|\frac{U_{0,n}}{s_{00,n}}\right| + \frac{\ell_{1,n}}{L_n} \left|\frac{U_{1,n}}{s_{11,n}}\right| \right] > \epsilon \right) \\ &\quad+ \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}_\theta\left(|\overline D_{1,n}-p_1|>\underline p/2\right). \end{aligned} \]

The second term on the right-hand side converges to zero by Lemma 1. For the first term, observe that for each \(d \in \{0,1\}\),

\[ \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[\frac{U_{d,n}}{s_{dd,n}}\right]=0, \qquad \mathbb{V}_\theta\left(\frac{U_{d,n}}{s_{dd,n}}\right) = \frac{p_0p_1}{n}. \]

Thus \(U_{d,n}/s_{dd,n}=o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\) by Chebyshev’s inequality. The weights \(\ell_{0,n}/L_n\) and \(\ell_{1,n}/L_n\) lie in \([0,1]\), so the bracketed term in (32) is also \(o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\). By Lemma 1, \(\sqrt n(\overline D_{1,n}-p_1)=O_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\). Therefore, by Proposition 1, the product inside the first probability on the right-hand side is \(o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\). Thus

\[ \frac{R_n}{\widetilde{\sigma}_n}=o_{\mathcal{P}}(1), \]

which by (31) proves (13).

Derivation of (15)

The bound in (8) gives \(0\leq\omega_n^2\leq1\). Moreover, \(|D_i-p_1|\leq1\) and the definition of \(\widetilde{\sigma}_n\) imply \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{|X_{i,n}|}{\widetilde\sigma_n} &\leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{p_0p_1}} \frac{ \dfrac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{p_0} \dfrac{|u_{i,0,n}|}{\sqrt n\,s_{00,n}} + \dfrac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{p_1} \dfrac{|u_{i,1,n}|}{\sqrt n\,s_{11,n}} }{ \dfrac{|a_{0,n}|s_{00,n}}{p_0} + \dfrac{|a_{1,n}|s_{11,n}}{p_1} } \\ &\leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{\underline p(1-\underline p)}} \max_{d\in\{0,1\}} \frac{|u_{i,d,n}|}{\sqrt n\,s_{dd,n}}. \end{aligned} \tag{33}\] Define the uniform envelope \[ \eta_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\underline p(1-\underline p)}} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \max_{d\in\{0,1\}} \max_{1\leq i\leq n} \frac{|u_{i,d,n}|}{\sqrt n\,s_{dd,n}}. \] Then \(|X_{i,n}|/\widetilde\sigma_n\leq\eta_n\) almost surely for every \(i\) and \(\theta\in\Theta\), and Assumption 3 gives \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \eta_n=0. \tag{34}\]

Now consider arbitrary sequences \(n_k\to\infty\) and \(\theta_k\in\Theta\). Since \(\omega_{n_k}^2\in[0,1]\), there is a further subsequence \(n_{k_j}\) along which \(\omega_{n_{k_j}}^2\to\omega^2\) for some \(\omega^2\in[0,1]\). If \(\omega>0\), then along this further subsequence, \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\omega_{n_{k_j}}^3} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}} \left| \frac{X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \right|^3 &\leq \frac{\eta_{n_{k_j}}}{\omega_{n_{k_j}}^3} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}} \left( \frac{X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \right)^2 \\ &= \frac{\eta_{n_{k_j}}}{\omega_{n_{k_j}}}. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\lim_{j\to\infty}\eta_{n_{k_j}}=0\) and \(\lim_{j\to\infty}\omega_{n_{k_j}}=\omega>0\), it follows that \[ \lim_{j\to\infty} \frac{1}{\omega_{n_{k_j}}^3} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}} \left| \frac{X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \right|^3 =0. \tag{35}\] The Lyapunov central limit theorem applied after normalization by \(\sigma_{n_{k_j}}=\omega_{n_{k_j}}\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}\), followed by \(\omega_{n_{k_j}}\to\omega\), therefore gives \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \rightsquigarrow N(0,\omega^2). \] If \(\omega=0\), then \[ \lim_{j\to\infty} \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}} \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}} \right)^2 = \lim_{j\to\infty}\omega_{n_{k_j}}^2 =0, \] so Chebyshev’s inequality gives \((\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}X_{i,n_{k_j}})/\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}\to0\) in probability. In either case, \(Z_{n_{k_j}}\rightsquigarrow\omega Z\) for \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), and the normalized sum \((\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}X_{i,n_{k_j}})/\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}\) converges weakly to the same limit. Part (ii) of Proposition 3 therefore gives \[ \lim_{j\to\infty} \sup_{g\in\mathcal G} \left| \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}}\left[ g\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k_j}}X_{i,n_{k_j}}}{\widetilde\sigma_{n_{k_j}}}\right) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k_j}}[g(Z_{n_{k_j}})] \right| =0 \] along this further subsequence. If the approximation \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^nX_{i,n}}{\widetilde\sigma_n} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\approx} Z_n \tag{36}\] failed, some sequences \(n_k\to\infty\) and \(\theta_k\in\Theta\) would keep this distance bounded away from zero, contradicting the existence of the further subsequence just constructed.

Furthermore, \[ \mathbb{E}_\theta \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^nX_{i,n}}{\widetilde\sigma_n} \right)^2 =\omega_n^2\leq1, \] so \((\sum_{i=1}^nX_{i,n})/\widetilde\sigma_n=O_{\mathcal{P}}(1)\) by Chebyshev’s inequality. By (12) and Proposition 5, \[ \frac{\widetilde\sigma_n}{\widehat\sigma_n} \stackrel{\mathcal{P}}{\to}1. \] Therefore Proposition 1 gives \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^nX_{i,n}}{\widehat\sigma_n} - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^nX_{i,n}}{\widetilde\sigma_n} =o_{\mathcal{P}}(1). \tag{37}\] Equations (36) and (37), together with Proposition 4, give (15).
