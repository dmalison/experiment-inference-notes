Regularity Diagnostics

Author

David Malison

Last modified

In our note on treatment group means, we constructed confidence intervals that are uniformly valid under a regularity condition that rules out dominant units. Here, we develop a finite-sample diagnostic for assessing whether that condition is plausible.

Variance-share diagnostic

We use the notation from the treatment group means note. For \(d\in\{0,1\}\), define \[ \kappa_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n} \frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}u_{i,d,n}^2}{\,s_{dd,n}^2}. \]

Since \(s_{dd,n}^2=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n u_{i,d,n}^2\), we can equivalently write

\[ \kappa_{d,n} = \frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}u_{i,d,n}^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n u_{i,d,n}^2}. \]

Thus, \(\kappa_{d,n}\) is the largest share of variation in the sequence of potential outcomes under treatment \(d\) attributable to any single unit. Let \[ \kappa_n = \max\{\kappa_{0,n},\kappa_{1,n}\}. \] The negligibility condition in Assumption 3 is equivalent to requiring

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\theta \in \Theta}\kappa_{n} =0. \]

Thus, if \(\kappa_n\) is large in a finite sample, the asymptotic approximations that rely on Assumption 3 may be unreliable.

Because only one potential outcome is observed for each unit, \(\kappa_n\) cannot be computed directly. A feasible sample analogue uses observed within-arm residuals in place of the unobserved potential-outcome deviations. For \(d\in\{0,1\}\), let \[ n_d=n\overline{D}_{d,n} \] denote the number of units assigned to treatment \(d\). Define \[ \widehat\kappa_{d,n} = \frac{1}{n_d}\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n} |Y_i-\widehat{y}_{d,n}|^2 D_{i,d}}{\widehat{s}_{dd,n}^2}, \tag{1}\] and let \[ \widehat\kappa_n = \max\{\widehat\kappa_{0,n},\widehat\kappa_{1,n}\}. \] This statistic is easy to compute and has the same intuitive variance-share interpretation as \(\kappa_n\). A warning can be triggered when \(\widehat\kappa_n\) exceeds a maximum tolerance \(\overline\kappa\).

Choosing the maximum tolerance

To motivate a choice of \(\overline\kappa\), suppose the strong null holds:

\[ y_{i,1}=y_{i,0}=y_i \qquad \mathrm{for\ every}\ i. \tag{2}\]

Define unit \(i\)’s share of the total outcome variation as

\[ q_{i,n} = \frac{(y_i-\overline y)^2} {\sum_{j=1}^n(y_j-\overline y)^2}, \qquad \overline y=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i. \]

Also define the ratio

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_n &= \frac{1}{n^{3/2}} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n|y_i-\overline y|^3}{s_n^3} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n q_{i,n}^{3/2}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(s_n^2=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\overline y)^2\). Because \(\max_i q_{i,n}=\kappa_n\), \(\sum_i q_{i,n}=1\), and \(q_{i,n}\leq\kappa_n\) for every unit, we have

\[ \kappa_n^{3/2} \leq \lambda_n \leq \sqrt{\kappa_n}\sum_{i=1}^n q_{i,n} = \sqrt{\kappa_n}. \tag{3}\]

Now let \(\Phi\) denote the standard normal distribution function. Define the ATE, its estimator, and its conservative standard-error scale by

\[ \psi_n^\mathrm{ATE} = \overline y_{1,n}-\overline y_{0,n}, \qquad \widehat\psi_n^\mathrm{ATE} = \widehat y_{1,n}-\widehat y_{0,n}, \qquad \widetilde\sigma_n^\mathrm{ATE} = \frac{s_n}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}. \]

Also define the linearization

\[ T_n = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^n(D_i-p)(y_i-\overline y) }{ \sqrt{n p(1-p)}\,s_n }. \]

Under the strong null, we can show

\[ \sqrt n\frac{\widehat\psi_n^\mathrm{ATE}-\psi_n^\mathrm{ATE}}{\widetilde\sigma_n^\mathrm{ATE}} = T_n+o_{\mathcal P}(1). \]

The Berry–Esseen theorem gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \left| \mathbb P_\theta(T_n\leq t) -\Phi(t) \right| &\leq C \frac{p^2+(1-p)^2}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} \lambda_n \\ &\leq C \frac{p^2+(1-p)^2}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} \sqrt{\kappa_n}, \end{aligned} \tag{4}\] where \(C\) is a universal constant. Thus, as a heuristic for assessing the normal approximation to the standardized ATE estimator under the strong null, we can choose \(\overline\kappa\) so that the Berry–Esseen bound in (4) is no greater than a desired tolerance \(\overline\delta\) whenever \(\kappa_n\leq\overline\kappa\). It suffices to set

\[ \overline\kappa = \left( \frac{\overline\delta}{C} \frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{p^2+(1-p)^2} \right)^2. \tag{5}\]

Simulation

To illustrate the diagnostic’s finite-sample behavior, we simulate the sampling distribution of the ATE estimator under two different populations of potential outcomes. In both cases, the strong null holds, \(n=1{,}000\), and \(p=0.5\). Using \(\overline\delta=0.10\) and the upper bound \(C\leq0.5583\) from Shevtsova (2013), (5) gives the calibrated tolerance

\[ \overline\kappa = \left(\frac{0.10}{0.5583}\right)^2 \approx 0.032. \tag{6}\]
